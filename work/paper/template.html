Which page should an editor fix first? A refresh-priority model on real search data

Lane 2 — Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring · Capstone research paper
Built on the FlyRank ML Internship dataset · repo: Semedw/flyrank-ml-internship-starter

Abstract

Which page should an editor review first this week? We answer that question with a ranked review queue built on the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release ({{N_CLIENTS}} pseudonymized clients, ~79M daily rows, Jan 2025 – Jun 2026). We score each page at four month-end decision moments using only the 30 days of search signals known before the decision, and we label each page by an observed future outcome: whether its clicks (or impressions, for low-click pages) fell below 70% of their prior level in the next 30 days. A random forest trained on those feature-window signals and evaluated once, blind, on a sealed final test month ranked review candidates with {{RF_AUC}} ROC-AUC and {{RF_P50}} precision@50 — a {{LIFT_P50}} lift over the transparent rule baseline's {{BL_P50}} — at a {{BASE_RATE}} decline base rate. The output is a decision-support queue of pages with scores, actions, and reason codes, for editors triaging a fixed weekly review capacity.

1. Introduction / problem statement

Content teams face a familiar constraint: the queue of pages that might need a refresh, rewrite, or prune is far longer than any editor's weekly capacity. Someone has to decide which pages get the limited review hours first. Done badly, that triage wastes the hours on pages that were fine, and misses the pages whose visibility is actually collapsing.

This work supports exactly that decision. The unit of analysis is one content page at one decision moment. The output is a ranked queue: for every scoreable page, a review-priority score, a suggested action, and human-readable reason codes. The cost of a wrong call is bounded — a misranked page costs review time, not money — which is why a review queue is the right shape of output, and why precision on the top of the queue is the metric that matters.

Why does data/ML help at all? The signals that plausibly predict a coming decline — position, exposure, freshness, engagement, content depth — are many, tangled, and shifting over time. A hand-written rule (the baseline) can name the obvious cases, but whether the tangle of signals carries extra signal beyond that rule is a question the data can answer better than a person eyeballing it.

2. Data

Release: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703, Hugging Face, FlyRank/internship-warehouse), gated, read-token access. Everything is pseudonymized: client, content, and query identifiers are scrambled hashes; no client names, domains, URLs, titles, or raw queries exist in the release.

Tables and windows

  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily rows per content item (2025-01-27 → 2026-06-30), the source of all feature and label signals. We touch only the month=2026-01 … 2026-06 partitions the contract's windows fall in.
  • dim_content — content metadata (word/char counts, content age) joined for depth and freshness features.
  • dim_clients — per-client tracking start (gsc_data_start) used to filter clients whose history began after a decision moment.

Decision moments: four month-ends — 2026-02-28, 2026-03-31, 2026-04-30 (training months) and 2026-05-31 (the sealed test decision). For each, features use the 30 days before the moment and the label uses the 30 days after it. June 2026 — the panel's final month — is the test-label window and was never read during development; the _sample table (also June) was never used, precisely because it is the outcome window of the final decision.

What we excluded, and why

  • Label-derived fields (trend_direction, trend_pct and friends): they are computed from the outcome we are predicting.
  • Product decision flags (health_score, priority_score, action_type): they do not ship in the release — the data is observable signals only, and modeling the product's own answers would be circular.
  • The query table (fact_content_query_90d): its fixed 90-day window cannot be aligned leak-safely with our 30-day feature windows, so it is excluded entirely.
  • Identity fields (*_hash_id): used for grouping and splitting only, never as features.
  • Rows before a client's tracking start (per gsc_data_start): "no data yet" must not be read as "no traffic". GA4-derived columns are likewise gated by the release's ga4_data_available flag, with explicit has-signal flags.
  • Pages without evidence: no impressions in the feature window means nothing to score; such rows are excluded from supervised learning and kept as low-evidence monitor items.

3. Methodology

Assumptions

  • Decline is an observable future outcome, not a product's opinion: volume in the next 30 days vs the previous 30 days.
  • Minimum volume floors make the label robust to noise; they are policy choices, documented below and sensitivity-checked.
  • We claim only observed / directional / decision-support results. No causal claim: this data cannot prove that refreshing a page causes recovery.

Label definition (one sentence)

A page is decline_30d = 1 if its clicks in the 30 days after the decision moment fell below 70% of its prior-30-day clicks (clicks floor ≥ 30), or — for low-click pages — its impressions fell below 70% of prior-30-day impressions (impressions floor ≥ 300). Pages meeting neither floor are unlabelled (monitor bucket). The label is always computed from the label window, never the feature window. Across the four decision moments, {{N_EVIDENCE}} pages carry enough evidence to be labelled; the rest are kept as low-evidence monitor items.

Features (feature window only, known before the decision)

  • Exposure: log impressions, log clicks, days-with-impressions / days-with-clicks shares, log GA4 sessions, log AI sessions, log scroll events (each gated by data availability flags).
  • Position: average position (rows with no position data excluded), days-with-position share, position in the 1–20 review band.
  • Capture: CTR% (clicks/impressions, computed, not shipped), GA4 engagement share.
  • Content: word count, char count, content age, has-data flags for each gated field.

Identities (client_hash_id, content_hash_id) are never features; dates are never features; nothing from the label window (*_lw) is ever a feature.

Baseline (the thing to beat)

A transparent, hand-written rule scored entirely from feature-window signals: weighted normalized sub-scores for freshness risk (30%), CTR gap vs expected-by-position (30%), visibility (25%), and engagement depth (15%), with reason codes (aging_visible, stale_visible, ctr_fix_candidate, thin_visible, page_one_decay_risk, monitor). Every threshold in it is written in the code and stated in the repo.

Validation design

  • Time-aware split: train on the three earlier decisions (labels Mar/Apr/May), test once on the 2026-05-31 decision (labels June — the sealed month). The test set was never touched during development; the sealed test was evaluated exactly once.
  • No hyperparameter tuning: default sklearn settings, random_state=42, so nothing was selected by peeking at the test.
  • Client-holdout robustness: a GroupShuffleSplit over clients, inside the training months only, checks whether the pattern survives unseen sites (random forest median {{GSS_MED_AUC}} ROC-AUC, range {{GSS_MIN_AUC}}–{{GSS_MAX_AUC}} — the pattern does not depend on knowing the test clients).
  • Leakage checks (all pass): feature window and label window are disjoint by construction ([D-30, D) vs [D, D+30)); no *_lw column appears in the feature list; no hash column appears in the feature list; no June rows exist in training.

What the errors look like

The model's misses concentrate in two places: low-signal pages where the feature window was thin (few impressions days, near the floors), and pages whose decline arrived as a single large drop in the last days of the label window — visible after the fact, unlearnable from the feature window. Both are honest failure modes; both argue for keeping the model's output as a review queue rather than an automatic action.

5. Limitations & honest framing

  • Observed, not causal. This work shows that feature-window signals precede declines and rank pages. It does not show that acting on the queue (refresh, rewrite, prune) causes recovery — that would need an experiment.
  • Not a Google-algorithm claim. We never observe the search engine's internals; we only observe search-impressions, clicks, and positions.
  • One sealed month. The test is a single month on a single snapshot. Seasonality, SERP-layout changes, and client mix drift all could shift these numbers; the client-holdout robustness check is a guard, not a guarantee.
  • Label is a threshold choice. "70% of prior volume" is a defensible but arbitrary line; moving it changes the base rate ({{BASE_RATE_PCT}} of scored pages here) and the ranking's meaning (sensitivity is reported in the repo).
  • Decision-support only. Every high-ranked page still needs a human look: the data cannot see title quality, intent mismatch, or editorial context.
  • GA4 signals are sparser. Engagement features only exist where GA4 tracking was live (ga4_data_available); has-flags encode that, but the signal is thinner than search exposure.

6. Ranked recommendations (the action playbook)

For an editor with a fixed weekly review capacity, the queue is read top-down. Each page carries a score, an action, and reason codes. Recommended actions:

Rank bandActionWhenReason codes that fire
Top 5%Review firstHigh model score + ≥500 impressions stale_visible, ctr_fix_candidate, page_one_decay_risk
Next 10%Refresh or rewriteDeclining, still visible, thin or aging aging_visible, thin_visible
Mid queueMetadata reviewVisible, low CTR vs position tier ctr_fix_candidate
BottomMonitorNo action code or low evidence monitor

Human review & the no-go list

  • A person must check the page before any change — the model ranks, it does not edit.
  • Never automate pruning or merging from the queue alone; consolidation look-alikes need a human to confirm.
  • Never treat "declining" as "refresh will fix it" — the data supports prioritization, not treatment effect.
  • Re-run the queue monthly; retrain when the sealed-month evaluation drifts (monitoring triggers are in the repo).
